Cold & Cough: When Do You Need Medical Advice?

Written and medically reviewed by Dr. Bhagirath Vagh, MBBS, General Physician. Registration No. G-82680.
Last reviewed: September 20, 2026

A runny or blocked nose, sneezing, sore throat and cough are common symptoms of respiratory infections. In many cases, these symptoms are caused by viruses and improve without specific treatment. However, some symptoms can indicate a more serious infection or worsening of an existing medical condition.

This guide explains common causes of cold and cough symptoms, what you can usually do at home, and when it is appropriate to seek medical advice.

Important: Cold-like symptoms can occur with several respiratory infections, including influenza and COVID-19. Symptoms alone may not always tell you which infection you have. People at higher risk of severe illness may need earlier medical assessment or testing.

What causes a cold and cough?

The common cold is usually a viral infection of the upper respiratory tract. Many different respiratory viruses can cause cold symptoms, with rhinoviruses being among the most common causes.

Cough can occur because of irritation in the throat or airways, mucus draining from the nose into the throat, or inflammation caused by a respiratory infection.

Other conditions can also cause cough or cold-like symptoms, including allergies, asthma, influenza, COVID-19 and some other respiratory infections. The appropriate evaluation depends on the symptoms, duration, medical history and examination when required.

Common symptoms

A typical cold may cause:

  • Runny nose
  • Blocked or stuffy nose
  • Sneezing
  • Sore or scratchy throat
  • Cough
  • Headache
  • Mild body aches
  • Feeling tired or unwell
  • Sometimes a low-grade fever

Symptoms often become most noticeable during the first few days. Nasal symptoms and cough can sometimes continue for longer even after the infection itself is improving.

What can you do at home?

Most uncomplicated colds improve with supportive care. Useful measures can include:

  • Get adequate rest and sleep.
  • Drink enough fluids to maintain hydration.
  • Use saline nasal drops or spray for nasal congestion if helpful.
  • Warm fluids may soothe an irritated throat.
  • Gargling with warm salt water may help relieve a sore throat.
  • A clean humidifier may help some people with congestion.
  • Avoid cigarette smoke and other respiratory irritants.

Over-the-counter medicines may relieve particular symptoms in some adults, but they do not cure a viral cold. Check the active ingredients carefully, especially when using combination cold medicines, because more than one product may contain the same pain-relieving medicine.

Do not automatically use antibiotics for a cold. Antibiotics treat certain bacterial infections, not the viruses that cause the common cold. Taking antibiotics when they are not needed can cause side effects and contributes to antibiotic resistance.

Does yellow or green mucus mean you need antibiotics?

Not necessarily. Nasal mucus can naturally become thicker and change from clear to white, yellow or green during a cold. Mucus colour by itself does not establish that an infection is bacterial or that antibiotics are required.

When should you see a doctor?

Medical advice is appropriate when symptoms are severe, persistent, worsening, or concerning to you.

Consider contacting a healthcare professional if:

  • You have difficulty breathing or unusually fast breathing.
  • You become dehydrated or cannot maintain adequate fluid intake.
  • A fever persists for several days.
  • Symptoms continue for more than about 10 days without improvement.
  • Your fever or cough improves and then returns or becomes worse.
  • An existing chronic condition becomes worse.
  • Your symptoms are severe or you are concerned about them.

The appropriate threshold can vary according to age, pregnancy, medical history, medications and other risk factors.

When is a cold or cough an emergency?

Seek urgent emergency medical care if respiratory symptoms are accompanied by warning signs such as:

  • Severe difficulty breathing or shortness of breath
  • Persistent chest pain or pressure
  • New confusion or inability to stay awake
  • Seizures
  • Severe weakness or marked unsteadiness
  • Signs of significant dehydration, such as very little or no urine
  • Symptoms that improve and then suddenly become substantially worse

These are examples of emergency warning signs and are not a complete list. If you believe you are experiencing a medical emergency, seek immediate in-person emergency care rather than relying on an online consultation.

Who may need earlier medical advice?

A respiratory infection can be more serious in people with certain underlying conditions or risk factors. Earlier medical advice may be appropriate for people with conditions such as:

  • Asthma or chronic lung disease
  • Significant heart disease
  • Diabetes or other conditions affecting overall health
  • A weakened immune system
  • Other chronic medical conditions that may worsen during infection

Older adults and people at higher risk of severe influenza or COVID-19 may also benefit from contacting a healthcare professional early when respiratory symptoms begin, because some antiviral treatments work best when started soon after symptoms begin.

Cold, flu or COVID-19?

A common cold, influenza and COVID-19 can share symptoms such as cough, sore throat, congestion, headache and tiredness. Symptoms alone may not reliably distinguish them.

If you are at increased risk of severe illness, or if your symptoms are significant or unusual, a healthcare professional can help determine whether testing or further evaluation is appropriate.

How long does a cold usually last?

Many colds improve within about a week, although some symptoms, particularly cough or nasal congestion, can last longer. CDC guidance notes that uncomplicated colds usually improve on their own, while symptoms lasting more than 10 days without improvement or symptoms that worsen after initially improving warrant medical attention.

How can you reduce the spread of a cold?

  • Wash your hands regularly with soap and water.
  • Cover coughs and sneezes.
  • Avoid close contact with others when you are unwell.
  • Improve ventilation or cleaner indoor air when possible.
  • Avoid sharing cups, utensils and other personal items while sick.
  • Follow appropriate respiratory infection precautions when around people at higher risk.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need antibiotics for a cold?

Usually no. The common cold is generally caused by viruses, and antibiotics do not treat viral infections. Antibiotics may be appropriate when a healthcare professional identifies a bacterial infection that requires them.

Can a cough continue after a cold?

Yes. Cough can sometimes persist after other cold symptoms improve. A cough that is severe, worsening, associated with breathing difficulty or chest pain, or lasting longer than expected should be medically assessed.

Is green mucus always a bacterial infection?

No. Nasal mucus can change colour during a viral cold. Colour alone does not determine whether antibiotics are needed.

Should I see a doctor for every cough?

Not necessarily. Many uncomplicated coughs associated with viral respiratory infections improve with time. However, severe symptoms, breathing difficulty, chest pain, prolonged symptoms, worsening after initial improvement, or relevant underlying medical conditions should prompt medical advice.

Can I get medical advice online for a cold or cough?

Some non-emergency cold and cough concerns may be suitable for an online consultation. A doctor may review your symptoms, duration, medical history and medications and determine whether online management is appropriate or whether an in-person examination is needed.
