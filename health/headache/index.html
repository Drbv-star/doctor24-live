Headache: Common Causes & Warning Signs You Shouldn't Ignore

Written and medically reviewed by Dr. Bhagirath Vagh, MBBS, General Physician. Registration No. G-82680.
Last reviewed: September 20, 2026

Headaches are very common and most are not caused by a serious condition. They can occur because of stress, dehydration, missed meals, infections, migraine and many other factors. However, a new, sudden or unusually severe headache can sometimes require urgent medical assessment.

This guide explains common headache causes, simple measures that may help, and warning signs that should not be ignored.

Important: The pattern of a headache matters. A familiar, mild headache may have a very different significance from a sudden severe headache or a headache accompanied by neurological symptoms.

What can cause a headache?

Common causes and triggers include:

  • Stress or emotional tension
  • Not drinking enough fluids
  • Skipping meals
  • Lack of sleep or changes in sleep
  • Cold, flu or other infections
  • Alcohol
  • Eye strain or vision problems
  • Poor posture or muscle tension
  • Migraine
  • Some medicines or frequent use of pain-relieving medicines

Headache can also occur with medical conditions that require assessment. The cause cannot always be determined from the location or intensity of pain alone.

Common types of headache

Tension-type headache

Tension-type headaches are common and may feel like pressure or tightening around the head. Stress, muscle tension and other factors can contribute.

Migraine

Migraine can cause moderate to severe headache and may be accompanied by nausea or increased sensitivity to light or sound. Some people experience visual or other neurological symptoms called an aura.

Headache associated with infection

Headache can occur during a cold, flu or other infection, particularly when fever or other symptoms are present.

Other causes

Less common but important causes include problems involving blood vessels in the brain, bleeding, infections such as meningitis, head injury and other neurological conditions. These possibilities become more important when a headache has unusual features or occurs with other warning symptoms.

What can you do for a mild headache?

If your headache is mild and similar to headaches you have experienced before, simple measures may help:

  • Drink enough water.
  • Eat regular meals and avoid prolonged fasting.
  • Get adequate sleep and rest.
  • Try to relax if stress appears to be contributing.
  • Reduce prolonged screen or eye strain where possible.
  • Avoid alcohol while you have a headache.

Some adults may use an appropriate over-the-counter pain reliever, such as paracetamol or ibuprofen, when it is safe for them to do so. Follow the product instructions and consider your other medicines, allergies, pregnancy status and medical conditions before taking medication.

Be careful with frequent painkiller use. Regularly needing pain medicines several days each week can itself contribute to recurrent headaches and should be discussed with a healthcare professional.

When should you see a doctor?

Consider medical assessment if:

  • Your headaches keep coming back.
  • Your usual headache pattern changes.
  • Your headaches are becoming more severe or frequent.
  • Pain-relieving medicines no longer help as they previously did.
  • A headache lasts for several days.
  • Headaches repeatedly wake you from sleep.
  • You develop headaches for the first time later in adulthood.
  • You need pain medicines frequently to control headaches.
  • You have persistent headaches together with other symptoms that concern you.

Headache warning signs: when is it an emergency?

Seek emergency medical care immediately if a headache:

  • Starts suddenly and is extremely severe or explosive.
  • Is the worst or one of the most severe headaches you have ever experienced.
  • Occurs with new weakness or numbness, especially on one side of the body.
  • Occurs with difficulty speaking, understanding speech, walking or maintaining balance.
  • Occurs with sudden vision loss or major visual disturbance.
  • Occurs with confusion, loss of consciousness or a seizure.
  • Occurs with a high fever and stiff neck.
  • Develops after a significant head injury.
  • Is accompanied by repeated vomiting or rapidly worsening symptoms.

These warning signs can occur with conditions such as stroke, bleeding around the brain, meningitis or other serious disorders. They require urgent in-person assessment rather than an online consultation.

Headache with fever and stiff neck

Headache together with fever and a stiff neck can be a sign of meningitis. Other symptoms can include vomiting, sensitivity to light and confusion. Meningitis can be serious, and someone with these symptoms should receive urgent medical assessment.

Headache and stroke warning signs

A sudden severe headache can sometimes occur with stroke. More important warning signs include sudden weakness or numbness on one side, difficulty speaking, sudden vision problems, dizziness or loss of balance.

If these symptoms occur suddenly, treat them as an emergency and seek immediate emergency medical care. Do not wait for an online consultation.

Headache after a head injury

A new or severe headache following a significant head injury requires medical assessment. Seek urgent care particularly if the headache is worsening or accompanied by vomiting, confusion, drowsiness, weakness, seizures or loss of consciousness.

What information can help a doctor evaluate a headache?

If you are seeking medical advice, it can be useful to note:

  • When the headache started
  • Whether it began suddenly or gradually
  • Where the pain is located
  • How severe the pain is
  • How long each episode lasts
  • How often headaches occur
  • Other symptoms such as nausea, vomiting or visual changes
  • Possible triggers such as missed meals, stress or lack of sleep
  • What medicines you have taken and whether they helped

Frequently asked questions

Are most headaches serious?

Most headaches are not caused by a serious condition. However, sudden severe headaches or headaches accompanied by neurological symptoms, fever with stiff neck, confusion, seizures or significant head injury require urgent assessment.

Can dehydration cause a headache?

Yes. Not drinking enough fluids can contribute to headaches. Staying adequately hydrated is one of the simple measures that may help.

Can stress cause headaches?

Yes. Stress and muscle tension can contribute to headaches, particularly tension-type headaches. Relaxation, adequate sleep and regular meals may help some people.

When is a headache an emergency?

A sudden extremely severe headache, a headache after significant head injury, or a headache accompanied by weakness, numbness, speech or vision problems, confusion, seizures, high fever with stiff neck, or other serious symptoms requires emergency medical assessment.

Can I get medical advice online for a headache?

Some non-emergency headache concerns may be suitable for an online consultation. A doctor can review the pattern, associated symptoms, medical history and medicines and determine whether online management is appropriate or whether an in-person examination is needed.
